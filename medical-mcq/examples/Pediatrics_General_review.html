Respiratory & Infection

Question 1 Bronchiolitis_Most_Common_Cause

RecallEasyKey length: Longest

Which of the following is the most common viral cause of acute bronchiolitis in infants?

  1. ✓ A)Respiratory syncytial virusKEY
  2. B)Human metapneumovirus
  3. C)Parainfluenza virus
  4. D)Influenza A virus
  5. E)Adenovirus
Rationale
RSV causes most cases of acute bronchiolitis in infants. Seasonal winter epidemics are typical.
Grounding (model-sourced)
Established pediatric epidemiology: RSV is the predominant cause of bronchiolitis, accounting for about 50–80% of cases. Human metapneumovirus, parainfluenza, influenza and adenovirus are recognized but less frequent causes. Each distractor is a true cause of bronchiolitis but not the most common one, so none competes for the key.

The key is unambiguous and all the options are real respiratory viruses of the same kind. Because it is a recall question it can't score 5, and the correct answer is the longest option (counted in the length balance).

Question 5 Febrile_Neonate_8_to_21_Days_Next_Step

VignetteChallengingKey length: Middle

A 16-day-old boy born at term is brought to the emergency department because he has a rectal temperature of 38.3 °C. He is feeding fairly well, is alert and looks well. Heart rate is 160/min, and capillary refill time is under 2 seconds. Examination finds no focus of infection. The pregnancy and delivery were uncomplicated.

Which of the following is the most appropriate next step in management?

  1. A)Urinalysis alone, then discharge with review in 24 hours if normal
  2. B)Oral amoxicillin and outpatient review within 48 hours
  3. C)Chest radiograph, with antibiotics only if consolidation is seen
  4. D)Urinalysis and blood culture, with lumbar puncture only if CRP is raised
  5. ✓ E)Urinalysis, blood and CSF cultures, then admission for IV antibioticsKEY
Rationale
Every febrile infant aged 8–21 days needs a full sepsis evaluation including lumbar puncture, followed by admission and parenteral antibiotics, however well they look.
Grounding (model-sourced)
AAP 2021 guideline on well-appearing febrile infants aged 8–60 days: for infants aged 8–21 days, do a urinalysis, blood culture and CSF analysis, admit, and start parenteral antimicrobials. NICE NG143 likewise advises a full workup including lumbar puncture under 1 month. Why the distractors are wrong: D, which uses inflammatory markers to decide on lumbar puncture, applies only from 22 days. A and B leave possible bacteremia or meningitis untreated. C is not indicated when there are no respiratory signs.
Confidence grading
Key: HighDistractors: HighOverall: 4/5
The options form a gradient: D is correct only for an older age band. Five days later this child would fall into the 22–28 day band, so the age cut-off is the point being tested, and students who skim may miss it.

Neonatology

Question 2 Physiological_Neonatal_Jaundice_Mechanism

RecallEasyKey length: Middle

Which of the following best explains physiological (non-pathological) unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia in a healthy, term, breastfed neonate on day 3 of life?

  1. A)Antibody-mediated hemolysis of fetal red cells
  2. B)Defective red cell membrane spectrin
  3. ✓ C)Immature hepatic bilirubin conjugationKEY
  4. D)Obstruction of the extrahepatic bile ducts
  5. E)Absent canalicular bilirubin export by MRP2
Rationale
Low UDP-glucuronosyltransferase (UGT1A1) activity in the newborn liver limits conjugation, so unconjugated bilirubin builds up in the first days of life.
Grounding (model-sourced)
Standard neonatal physiology: physiological jaundice is multifactorial (higher red cell mass, shorter red cell lifespan, increased enterohepatic circulation and immature UGT1A1 conjugation), and of these only immature conjugation is offered. Why the distractors are wrong: isoimmune hemolysis (A) and spherocytosis (B) are pathological causes. Biliary obstruction (D) and MRP2 deficiency (Dubin–Johnson, E) cause conjugated hyperbilirubinemia.
Confidence grading
Key: HighDistractors: HighOverall: 4/5
The key is clean. The distractors are distinct mechanisms. The stem says 'best explains' because the physiology has several contributing causes.

Gastroenterology & Fluids

Question 3 Gastroenteritis_Moderate_Dehydration_Initial_Management

VignetteStandardKey length: Middle

An 18-month-old boy weighing 11 kg has had watery, non-bloody diarrhea and occasional vomiting for 2 days. He is alert and irritable and drinks eagerly when offered fluids. Heart rate is 135/min. His mucous membranes are dry, tears are reduced, and capillary refill time is 2 seconds. A skin pinch retracts slowly. His dehydration is estimated at about 6%.

Which of the following is the most appropriate initial management?

  1. A)Intravenous 0.9% saline bolus of 20 mL/kg
  2. B)Diluted fruit juice and plain water as tolerated
  3. C)Loperamide and nothing by mouth for 12 hours
  4. ✓ D)Low-osmolarity ORS, about 75 mL/kg over 4 hoursKEY
  5. E)Nasogastric lactose-free formula on admission
Rationale
For some-to-moderate dehydration in an alert child who can drink, oral rehydration with low-osmolarity ORS is first line (WHO Plan B).
Grounding (model-sourced)
WHO Plan B and AAP/ESPGHAN guidance: give 50–100 mL/kg of ORS over 3–4 hours for mild-to-moderate dehydration. Why the distractors are wrong: an IV bolus (A) is for severe dehydration or shock, and this child has neither. Juice and plain water (B) are hyperosmolar or contain too little sodium. Antimotility drugs (D) are contraindicated in young children. Nasogastric feeding with formula (E) is not rehydration therapy and is not first line.
Confidence grading
Key: HighDistractors: HighOverall: 5/5
The vignette is classic and has enough findings to rule out severe dehydration. All the options are management steps, and none of the distractors can be defended as best.

Hematology & Nutrition

Question 4 Microcytic_Anemia_Cow_Milk_Diagnosis

VignetteStandardKey length: Middle

A 15-month-old girl is brought in because she looks pale and has been irritable for a month. She drinks about 1.2 L of cow's milk a day and eats little solid food. She is hemodynamically stable, and there is no hepatosplenomegaly.

Hemoglobin 8.1 g/dL (normal 11.0–13.5); MCV 61 fL (70–86); RBC count 3.9 × 10¹²/L; RDW 18% (11.5–14.5); serum ferritin 4 ng/mL (12–150).

Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. A)Beta-thalassemia trait
  2. ✓ B)Iron deficiency anemiaKEY
  3. C)Anemia of chronic disease
  4. D)Lead poisoning
  5. E)Sideroblastic anemia
Rationale
Drinking too much cow's milk, together with microcytosis, a high RDW, a Mentzer index above 13 (61/3.9 ≈ 15.6) and a ferritin below 12 ng/mL, establishes iron deficiency anemia.
Grounding (model-sourced)
Established hematology: a ferritin below 12 ng/mL is diagnostic of iron deficiency in children under 5 (WHO). More than about 700 mL of cow's milk a day is a classic cause in toddlers. Why the distractors are wrong: thalassemia trait (A) has a normal RDW and a Mentzer index below 13. Anemia of chronic disease (C) has a normal or high ferritin. Lead poisoning (D) and sideroblastic anemia (E) do not explain a very low ferritin.
Confidence grading
Key: HighDistractors: HighOverall: 4/5
The key is unambiguous. Note that lead poisoning can coexist with iron deficiency in toddlers. That doesn't change the best answer, but you could raise it in teaching.
